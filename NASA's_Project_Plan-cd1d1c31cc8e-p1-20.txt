ASTRA Junior: Become Mission-Ready
Complete Game-Making Guide for the “Build a Junior
Astronaut Mission Trainer” Challenge
1. Project overview
Game idea
ASTRA Junior is an interactive training game in which a student becomes a junior astronaut. The
player learns how a lunar or Martian outpost works, prepares equipment, makes mission
decisions, handles emergencies, explains those decisions, and receives a personalized
astronaut-training report.
The game should teach one central idea:
Every space-mission decision solves one problem while possibly creating another.
The game should not feel like a textbook. The player should learn by making choices, seeing
consequences, recovering from mistakes, and trying again.
Target audience
  Primary audience: students approximately 9–16 years old
  Secondary audience: teachers, parents, judges, and STEM mentors
  Interaction style: short missions, visual choices, simple explanations, repeatable challenges
  Recommended play session: 10–20 minutes for a short demo; 30–45 minutes for the full ten-
  level experience
Recommended platform
Build a browser-based game so judges can open it without installing special software.
Recommended beginner-friendly technology:
  HTML for page structure
  CSS for layout and visual design
  JavaScript for game rules
  Phaser 3 if you want animations, maps, and game scenes
  Local storage for saving progress and badges
A simple HTML/CSS/JavaScript version is sufficient for a strong prototype. Add Phaser only if
your team already knows it or needs richer animation.



2. Your unique identity
Many teams may build a game with resource bars. ASTRA Junior should stand out because it is
a trainer, not only a survival simulator.
Signature features
A. Mission Readiness Score
Evaluate the player across several skills:
  Safety awareness
  Resource management
  Scientific thinking
  Emergency response
  Communication
  Teamwork
  Sustainability
  Decision explanation
Do not reduce performance to one score. Give the player a clear skill profile.
B. Cause-and-effect map
When the player makes an important decision, show the chain of consequences:
Use emergency battery

Greenhouse remains active

Food production is protected

Backup power becomes lower

The next emergency becomes more dangerous
This visual map is one of the strongest ways to show engineering trade-offs.
C. Explain Your Decision
After major decisions, ask the player to select or write why they chose an action.
Example:
“I stopped the laboratory because keeping oxygen and water systems active was more important.”
Reward reasoning that identifies priorities, risks, and consequences—not only the choice that
produces the highest score.
D. Mission Memory
At the end of the game, create a personalized report:
  Mission duration
  Best decision
  Most dangerous decision
  Systems protected
  Systems neglected
  Strongest astronaut skill
  Recommended next training
  Unique mission title
Example title: The Careful Systems Thinker.



3. Core gameplay loop
Every level should follow the same simple loop so students understand how to play:
1. Briefing — Mission Control explains the objective.
2. Learn — The player receives a short explanation or interactive tutorial.
3. Prepare — The player chooses equipment, resources, or priorities.
4. Act — The player makes one or more mission decisions.
5. Consequence — Systems change and an event may occur.
6. Explain — The player explains the most important decision.
7. Review — The game shows what improved, what worsened, and why.
8. Advance — The player earns a badge, unlocks the next level, or repeats the level.
Keep each level focused on one main learning objective. Do not introduce every system at once.

4.2 Suggested simple rules
Use rules that are understandable rather than scientifically overwhelming.
Example per-turn changes:
oxygen = oxygen - crew_size * oxygen_use
water = water - crew_size * water_use
food = food - crew_size * food_use
power = power + power_generated - power_consumed
For a greenhouse:
if greenhouse_powered and greenhouse_water >= plant_water_cost:
   food = food + food_produced
   water = water - plant_water_cost
For an emergency:
if power < minimum_power:
   life_support_efficiency = life_support_efficiency - 1
   risk_level = risk_level + 1
Do not present the equations to young players unless you include an optional “Engineer Mode.”

4.4 Failure and recovery
Do not end the game immediately after one mistake. Use three states:
  Stable: The outpost is functioning normally.
  Warning: A system is at risk; the player can recover.
  Critical: The player must make an emergency decision.
Only end a mission when a critical system remains unsupported after the recovery opportunity.
Then show the cause of failure and allow a retry.



5. The ten levels
Level 1 — Welcome to Astronaut Training
Learning objective
Understand the basic outpost systems.
Player task
Identify which system is affected by each situation.
Content
Introduce oxygen, water, food, power, temperature, and communication.
Example interactions
  Broken air filter oxygen problem
  Empty battery power problem
  Water leak water problem
  Failed heater temperature problem
Success condition
The player correctly identifies at least 4 of 5 situations.
Failure recovery
Show a short explanation and allow the player to try again.
Badge
Systems Spotter
Implementation note
Use cards or icons rather than complex simulation. This level should take 2–3 minutes.



Level 2 — Choose Your Spacesuit
Learning objective
Understand preparation and equipment prioritization.
Player task
Prepare a spacesuit and mission kit for a short surface excursion.
Equipment choices
  Oxygen tank
  Radiation protection
  Thermal protection
  Communication device
  Tool kit
  Emergency battery
  Sample container
Give the player limited weight capacity, such as 5 equipment slots.
Mission scenario
The astronaut must collect a rock sample near the outpost and return safely.
Success condition
The player selects all essential equipment and returns before oxygen or safety becomes critical.
Main trade-off
More tools allow more science but increase weight and reduce flexibility.
Badge
Mission Preparer



Level 3 — Build Your Mission Kit
Learning objective
Plan supplies under limited storage and weight.
Player task
Pack supplies for the first outpost mission.
Available supplies
  Food
  Water
  Batteries
  Repair tools
  Medical supplies
  Seeds
  Scientific instruments
  Spare filters
Rules
Set both a storage limit and a mass limit. The player cannot take everything.
Success condition
The player packs enough supplies to cover the mission and at least one likely emergency.
Main trade-off
Carrying more emergency equipment may require sacrificing science equipment or food variety.
Badge
Resource Planner



Level 4 — Lunar Power Challenge
Learning objective
Understand energy generation, storage, and prioritization.
Player task
Keep a lunar base operating through a long low-sunlight period.
Systems
  Solar panels
  Batteries
  Heating
  Life support
  Lighting
  Communications
  Laboratory
Player decisions
  Which systems remain active?
  Should the laboratory be paused?
  Should battery power be reserved for life support?
  Should greenhouse lighting be reduced?
Success condition
Maintain life support and communication until sunlight returns.
Main trade-off
Scientific progress and food production compete with survival systems for power.
Badge
Power Guardian



Level 5 — Radiation and EVA Mission
Learning objective
Understand radiation risk, mission duration, and safety limits.
Player task
Send an astronaut outside to inspect equipment.
Player choices
  Select shielding level
  Select route
  Choose mission duration
  Decide whether to collect science samples
  Decide when to return
Event
A radiation warning appears during the surface activity.
Success condition
Complete the essential inspection without exceeding the safety limit.
Main trade-off
Staying longer can produce more science but increases risk.
Badge
Safety Specialist



Level 6 — Greenhouse and Water Mission
Learning objective
Understand sustainability and closed-loop resource systems.
Player task
Produce enough food while protecting the water supply.
Systems
  Greenhouse lighting
  Water recycling
  Plant selection
  Temperature control
  Food storage
  Oxygen production
Plant choices
  Fast-growing crop: produces food quickly but uses more water
  Efficient crop: uses less water but grows slowly
  Oxygen-producing plant: helps the air system but provides less food
Success condition
Keep the crew supplied for the target period without exhausting water.
Main trade-off
The best food system is not always the fastest-growing system.
Badge
Life-Support Farmer



Level 7 — Mars Communication Delay
Learning objective
Practice independent decision-making with incomplete information.
Player task
Respond to a developing problem before Mission Control can answer.
Possible problems
  Water leak
  Damaged rover
  Power warning
  Medical concern
  Computer sensor failure
Game mechanic
Mission Control messages arrive after a delay. The player must act using the information
currently available.
Success condition
Protect the most important system while avoiding unnecessary actions.
Main trade-off
Waiting may provide better information but allow the problem to become worse.
Badge
Independent Decision-Maker



Level 8 — Dust Storm Emergency
Learning objective
Respond to a major environmental event.
Player task
Protect the Martian outpost during a dust storm.
Available actions
  Save battery power
  Pause experiments
  Reduce greenhouse lighting
  Move the rover indoors
  Repair or clean solar panels
  Keep communication active
Success condition
Survive the storm and keep life support operating.
Main trade-off
The player cannot protect every system equally. They must prioritize essentials.
Badge
Storm Commander



Level 9 — Crew Health and Teamwork
Learning objective
Understand that astronauts are part of the mission system.
Player task
Create a schedule that balances work, exercise, sleep, science, maintenance, and personal time.
Events
  A crew member becomes exhausted
  A team disagreement delays a repair
  A medical issue requires attention
  An experiment deadline approaches
Player choices
  Reassign tasks
  Delay science work
  Schedule rest
  Request help from another crew member
  Use a robot for a simple task
Success condition
Complete the essential mission work while keeping crew health above the safe level.
Main trade-off
Working longer may complete more tasks now but reduce safety and performance later.
Badge
Crew Leader



Level 10 — Mission Commander Challenge
Learning objective
Combine all previous skills in a complete mission.
Scenario
A dust storm is approaching. The greenhouse needs power. An astronaut is outside repairing a
solar panel. The water recycler has stopped working. Mission Control cannot respond
immediately.
Player task
Prioritize actions, assign crew members, manage resources, and explain the reasoning behind
the most important decision.
Systems combined
  Oxygen
  Water
  Food
  Power
  Materials
  Radiation safety
  Communication delay
  Crew health
  Science objectives
  Emergency repair
Success condition
Complete the mission objectives while keeping the crew and critical systems safe.
Evaluation
The final level should consider both outcome and reasoning. A player who makes a safe decision
but loses some science should not automatically score worse than a risky player who earns
more science.
Badge
Junior Mission Commander



6. Scoring system
Use a transparent scoring model. Avoid mysterious points.
Suggested score categories
Each category can be scored from 0 to 100:
  Safety
  Resource management
  Planning
  Science
  Emergency response
  Teamwork
  Sustainability
  Decision explanation
Suggested weighting
final_score =
   safety * 0.25 +
   resource_management * 0.20 +
   emergency_response * 0.15 +
   teamwork * 0.15 +
   sustainability * 0.10 +
   science * 0.10 +
   decision_explanation * 0.05
For young students, show the result as categories and feedback rather than exposing the
formula.
Feedback example
Safety: Excellent
You protected life support before optional equipment.

Resource management: Good
You saved water but used too much backup power.

Next training goal:
Practice planning for a second emergency.

Decision-processing sequence
1. Player selects an action.
2. Show a confirmation screen.
3. Apply immediate resource changes.
4. Trigger the relevant event.
5. Apply delayed consequences if appropriate.
6. Add the decision to the mission log.
7. Ask the player to explain the decision.
8. Update skill scores.
9. Show the cause-and-effect explanation.
10. Check whether the level is complete.
Save progress
Use browser local storage:
localStorage.setItem(
  "astraJuniorProgress",
  JSON.stringify(gameState)
);
Always include a Reset Progress option for testing.



9. Art, sound, and accessibility
Visual style
Use a clean mission-control style:
  Dark navy or charcoal background
  Bright cyan for information
  Orange for warnings
  Green for stable systems
  Red for critical emergencies
  Large icons and readable labels
Use original illustrations or properly licensed assets. Keep an asset-credit file in the project.
Accessibility requirements
  Do not use color alone to communicate status.
  Add text labels such as “Warning” and “Critical.”
  Use readable font sizes.
  Provide subtitles for audio.
  Add keyboard navigation.
  Avoid flashing effects.
  Use simple language and explain technical terms.
  Make the game playable without sound.
Audio
Use sound sparingly:
  Soft confirmation sound for safe actions
  Warning tone for system problems
  Calm mission-control voice or text for briefings
  Optional ambient spacecraft sound
The game should still work if all audio is muted.

11. Testing plan
Test the game with at least three types of people:
Student testers
Ask:
  Did you know what to do?
  Which level was most enjoyable?
  Which word or icon was confusing?
  Did you understand why your decision had consequences?
Non-technical testers
Ask them to play without help. This identifies confusing instructions and interface problems.
Technical testers
Check:
  Buttons work after repeated clicks.
  Progress saves and loads.
  The game cannot produce impossible resource values.
  A player cannot skip required levels accidentally.
  The game works on different screen sizes.
  The game still works after refresh.
Balance testing
For each level, record:
  Completion time
  Number of retries
  Most common failed action
  Most confusing instruction
  Average score
If almost everyone fails, simplify the explanation. If everyone succeeds immediately, add a
meaningful trade-off.



12. How to prove originality
No project can guarantee that nobody will notice similarities to another project. The safest
approach is to demonstrate that your concept, writing, design, and implementation were
developed by your team.
Keep evidence
Save:
  Brainstorming sketches
  Dated design decisions
  Wireframes
  Early prototypes
  Testing notes
  Team contributions
  Version history
  Asset licenses and credits
Avoid copying
Do not copy:
  Another game’s interface
  Character designs
  Text or mission scripts
  Sound effects without permission
  Code without following its license
  A known game’s exact scoring and progression structure
Make the project recognizably yours
Your strongest original combination is:
1. Mission training rather than simple survival
2. Cause-and-effect system maps
3. Explain Your Decision reasoning
4. Mission Readiness skill profile
5. Mission Memory report
6. Ten-level progression from trainee to commander
If AI tools help with code, images, or writing, follow the event’s rules and disclose assistance
when required. Human team members should make the final design decisions, test the game,
edit the content, and understand the code they submit.
13. Final presentation plan
Use a three-minute demonstration.
Demonstration structure
First 20 seconds: Problem
Explain that students often learn space engineering as separate facts, but real missions require
decisions between competing needs.
Next 30 seconds: Your solution
Introduce ASTRA Junior as a training game where students become junior astronauts, make
decisions, explain their reasoning, and learn from consequences.
Next 90 seconds: Gameplay
Demonstrate one mission:
1. Show the briefing.
2. Show the resource dashboard.
3. Make a difficult choice.
4. Show the immediate effect.
5. Show the delayed consequence.
6. Explain the cause-and-effect map.
7. Show the readiness feedback.
Final 40 seconds: Why it matters
Explain that the game builds systems thinking, safety awareness, planning, communication, and
problem-solving through play.
Suggested closing sentence
ASTRA Junior does not ask students to memorize how astronauts work; it lets them practice
thinking like astronauts.



14. Minimum viable version
If your team has limited time, build this version first:
  One title screen
  One astronaut profile
  One dashboard
  Four resources: oxygen, water, food, power
  Levels 1, 4, 6, 8, and 10
  Three emergency events
  Explain Your Decision feature
  Mission Readiness report
  One original visual theme
Then add Levels 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 if time allows. A polished five-level prototype is stronger than an
unfinished ten-level game.
For the competition submission, however, present the full ten-level structure even if some levels
are simplified.
